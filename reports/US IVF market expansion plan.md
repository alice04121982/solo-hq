# Build CairnFertility's US side state by state

CairnFertility should enter the US as a **coverage-and-cost navigator for people the system leaves out**, launched at `/us` alongside the untouched UK site, starting with **California and New York**. What a US patient pays turns first on whether their employer plan is self-funded or fully insured, and only second on which state issued it. So the lead feature is a client-side **coverage checker** that asks those questions in that order, backed by a finder with native USD prices and SART/CDC success data. The opening is real: US IVF is roughly a **$6B market** with self-pay cycles of **$15k-$30k**, no HFEA-style regulator, a federal success-rate team that was eliminated in 2025, and no competitor that combines price, success rates and eligibility for solo mums, solo dads, two mums and two dads. The main risks are legal rather than technical. Per-lead or per-booking clinic fees can be criminal patient brokering in Florida, and fertility answers are "consumer health data" under Washington's My Health My Data Act, which has a private right of action. The site's current posture (no trackers, wizard answers kept in the browser) already covers most of the privacy problem. Revenue has to stay flat-fee and non-contingent, with rankings kept separate from payment. The codebase needs a market key and a new data model rather than a rewrite. The critical path runs through US data and a US-qualified reviewer, not engineering.

**How to read the figures.** The research proxy blocked full-page reads of most primary sources (cdc.gov, sart.org, asrm.org, resolve.org, dol.gov, federalregister.gov, fertilityiq.com, sec.gov and others). Figures marked **†** come from search-engine summaries of the linked page, not a full read. Verify each one against the primary source before it appears on the site. Unmarked web figures came from pages that were read, but they still need a date check before publication. Codebase facts cite repo files and are verified.

## A $6B market where price, not permission, is the barrier

The US is larger than the UK and more opaque. The latest CDC-validated year, 2022, recorded **435,426 ART cycles at 457 clinics, producing 98,289 infants, about 2.6% of US births**† ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/art/php/surveillance/index.html); [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/articles/how-many-ivf-babies-are-born-in-the-us/)). **184,423 of those cycles (about 42%) were freeze-all banking cycles**†, so a US calculator needs separate "freeze eggs/embryos" and "fresh transfer" flows. SART's 2024 data, released in 2026, counts **449,772 cycles at 364 member clinics and 100,158 babies**, the first year above 100,000†, with about 79% elective single embryo transfer ([ASRM](https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/press-releasesbulletins/for-the-first-time-more-than-100000-babies-born-through-ivf-in-the-u.s.-in-a-single-year/)). The two series measure different populations, so the site should label counts "CDC (2022)" or "SART (2024)" and never mix them. Analyst sizing puts US IVF at **$5.88B in 2025, rising to $8.38B by 2033**† ([Grand View](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/horizon/outlook/in-vitro-fertilization-market/united-states)). Definitions differ widely between analysts, so treat that as indicative.

Cost is the defining US problem. Base services run **$12k-$17k per cycle, medication $3k-$7k, all-in $15k-$30k**†, with an average near **$23,474**† ([GoodRx](https://www.goodrx.com/conditions/fertility/ivf-costs); [CareRoute](https://www.careroute.ai/blog/ivf-cost)). FertilityIQ puts the typical patient at **2.3-2.7 cycles and close to $50,000**† ([FertilityIQ](https://www.fertilityiq.com/fertilityiq/ivf-in-vitro-fertilization/costs-of-ivf)). Add-ons stack on top: PGT-A $3k-$6k per batch, FET $4k-$6.5k+, egg freezing $14k-$20k per cycle, donor eggs an extra $20k-$35k† ([CNY](https://www.cnyfertility.com/egg-freezing-cost/); [Ovu](https://ovu.com/fertility-insights/donor-costs-2026-what-you-will-pay-for-egg-sperm-embryo-donation)). The spread is large. Low-cost clinics such as LARC and New Direction quote **$5k-$7k**†, and Mexico **$4k-$8.2k**† ([Gaia](https://gaiafamily.com/en-us/learn/editorials/which-ivf-clinics-in-the-u-s-are-most-affordable); [Ovu Mexico](https://ovu.com/fertility-insights/ivf-in-mexico-2026-medical-tourism-costs-fertility-clinic-guide)). Two US-specific products belong in the calculator. The first is **multi-cycle refund programmes**, such as Shady Grove's Shared Risk, which is priced only through a counsellor and limited to self-pay patients ([Shady Grove](https://www.shadygrovefertility.com/refund-programs-for-infertility-treatment/)). The second is **TrumpRx**: EMD Serono's Gonal-f, Ovidrel and Cetrotide at up to 84% off, which clinics say cuts self-pay medication to **$1,500-$3,500 from $4,000-$6,000**† ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/10/16/politics/trump-ivf-emd-serono-trumprx-drug-price); [Fertility Centers of New England](https://www.fertilitycenter.com/fertility_cares_blog/trumprx-for-fertility-medication/)). Sources disagree on TrumpRx's launch month (January vs February 2026), and the reported income-based eligibility comes only from secondary blogs. Offer it as a toggle once the rules are confirmed.

Prices are partly public. Kindbody, CNY and a few others publish price lists ([Kindbody](https://kindbody.com/services-pricing/)), but most clinics quote only after a consult. US price collection will therefore be a mix of scraping the minority who publish and dated, user- or clinic-submitted quotes. Ownership is also a real differentiator. **PE-affiliated clinics rose from 4% (2013) to 32% (2023) of clinics and ran 52% of 2022 cycles**, often behind local brands ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/private-equity-buyouts-fertility-consolidation); [RBEJ](https://rbej.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12958-025-01446-4)). **41.9% of women aged 15-50 live more than 30 minutes from a clinic** ([JARG 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10815-025-03680-6)). The finder therefore needs an owner/network field, a satellite-monitoring flag and distance search.

Success-rate data is the second structural difference. SART's clinic reports are about two years behind, require users to accept its terms, and carry a "do not compare clinics" disclaimer ([SART](https://www.sart.org/patients/fyi-videos/understanding-the-sart-clinic-report/)). CDC's NASS clinic datasets are public downloads ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/art/php/data-access/index.html)). As federal works they are the lower-risk licensing basis, though that is an inference to confirm. The catch is that **the CDC's entire six-person ART surveillance team was eliminated in April 2025**, leaving submission running (via a Westat contract to October 2027) but no clear publishing path ([Healthcare Brew](https://www.healthcare-brew.com/stories/2025/04/11/hhs-layoffs-reproductive-health-research); [NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/cdcs-ivf-team-gutted-even-trump-calls-fertilization-president-rcna199261)). The recommendation is to base per-clinic figures on CDC data, link every clinic to its SART report, ask SART for a display licence, show SART's own age bands, date-stamp everything, and **never rank clinics by success rate**, because patient-mix differences make such comparisons an FTC deception risk.

## Plan type decides coverage before the state does

There is no federal IVF mandate, and state mandates reach fewer people than their headlines suggest. Aggregators count **about 15 states plus DC requiring IVF coverage and about 10 more requiring lesser fertility cover**† ([Nava](https://www.navabenefits.com/resources/fertility-benefits-by-state-what-insurance-covers-ivf-and-where-coverage-is-mandated)). The "2026 guides" contradict each other, though. One lists West Virginia as an IVF state, although its law is HMO-only, and another claims Florida and Nevada mandates that nothing corroborates† ([doesinsurancecover](https://www.doesinsurancecover.com/blog/ivf-insurance-coverage-by-state); [Carrot](https://www.get-carrot.com/blog/does-insurance-cover-ivf)). The state table must be built from RESOLVE and statute text, not from aggregators.

The biggest limit is ERISA. **67% of covered workers, and 80% at larger firms, are in self-funded plans that state mandates cannot reach**, and 37% of workers at firms with 10-199 staff are in level-funded plans that generally escape them too ([KFF 2025](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2025-employer-health-benefits-survey/)). Most new mandates also stop at large, fully insured groups. **California's SB 729** (in force 1 January 2026 after the AB 116 delay) covers up to 3 retrievals and unlimited transfers, uses an inclusive definition that reaches single people and same-sex couples, and bans discrimination by orientation, gender identity and marital status. It applies only to fully insured groups of 101+ and excludes small-group, self-funded and religious employers ([Sequoia](https://www.sequoia.com/2025/07/california-mandates-infertility-ivf-coverage-updated/); [UCLA](https://law.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/PDFs/Center_on_Reproductive_Health/California%E2%80%99s%20SB%20729%20Expanding%20Access%20to%20IVF%20and%20Family-Building%20for%20All%20(1).pdf)). **Minnesota** (4 retrievals, 1 January 2026)†, the **DC benchmark** (2026)† and **Virginia's benchmark** (3 cycles, from 2028)† are the other recent additions ([MultiState](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2026/4/1/state-fertility-coverage-mandates-expand-in-2026-legislative-sessions-ivf-and-assisted-reproductive-technology-laws)). Older laws such as Texas's offer-only mandate (married, own gametes) define infertility in ways that shut out CairnFertility's core audience† ([ReproductiveFacts](https://www.reproductivefacts.org/patient-advocacy/state-and-territory-infertility-insurance-laws/)).

Employers and their vendors therefore matter more than legislatures. **47% of large employers covered IVF in 2024, 70% of those with 20,000+ staff, and 64% of covering employers extend it to people without a clinical infertility diagnosis** ([Mercer](https://www.mercer.com/en-us/about/newsroom/employers-enhanced-health-benefits-in-2024-adding-coverage-for-weight-loss-medications-and-ivf-despite-growing-health-cost/)). KFF's 27% for large firms† uses a different size cut, so the two figures should not be presented together ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/womens-health-policy/access-to-fertility-care-findings-from-the-2024-kff-womens-health-survey/)). Federal policy is moving through markets rather than mandates. The October 2025 FAQs (Part 72) let employers offer fertility cover as a standalone excepted benefit ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/faqs/aca-part-72)). A **May 2026 proposed rule would create "excepted fertility benefits" with a $120,000 lifetime cap from plan years starting 1 January 2027**, including self-insured versions. No final rule had been found as of September 2026 ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/13/2026-09479/excepted-fertility-benefits); [Foley](https://www.foley.com/p/102mvib/proposed-rule-would-bring-flexibility-to-employer-fertility-benefits/)). The public programmes are narrow. The TRICARE IVF expansion was cut from the final FY2026 NDAA ([19th News](https://19thnews.org/2025/12/2026-ndaa-defense-bill-military-spending/)). FEHB widened IVF for 2026† ([FedTools](https://www.fedtools.com/blog/fehb-fertility-coverage-2026)). Medicaid covers essentially no IVF outside narrow Utah genetic-carrier cases† ([RESOLVE](https://resolve.org/learn/financial-resources/insurance-coverage/medicaid-coverage-for-infertility-treatments-and-fertility-preservation/)). IVF counts as a deductible medical expense, but gestational surrogacy does not ([IRS Pub 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502)).

That ordering gives the **coverage checker** its logic. It asks, in order:

1. Where does your coverage come from: employer, marketplace, Medicaid, TRICARE, FEHB, or none?
2. For employer plans, is it self-funded, level-funded or fully insured? The checker explains how to tell from the SBC/SPD ("administered by" vs "insured by").
3. Which state issued the plan? This is not the same as where the user lives.
4. How many employees does the employer have?
5. Is it an HMO? This matters in Ohio, West Virginia and Montana.
6. Does the plan use a fertility vendor (Progyny, Carrot, Maven, Kindbody) or a standalone fertility benefit?
7. Does the plan's definition of infertility include single people and same-sex couples?

The output is always "the rule, its source and the date we checked it", never "you are covered". This follows the internal brief's instruction to stay at "here is the rule and here is the source" ([docs/international-strategy-brief.md:262-270](../docs/international-strategy-brief.md)).

## No HFEA: state law decides parentage, embryo status and who gets treated

UK readers need to be told plainly that the US has **no licensing regulator, no national donor register, no federal cap on donor offspring and no statutory storage limit**. Federal oversight covers donor infection screening under 21 CFR 1271 ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-L/part-1271/subpart-C)) and success-rate reporting under the 1992 Act. Everything else is left to voluntary ASRM/SART self-regulation and state law. For two dads and queer men, the FDA's January 2025 draft guidance would replace the time-based MSM deferral on anonymous tissue donation with individual risk questions, but **no evidence was found that it had been finalised** ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31524/recommendations-for-determining-eligibility-of-donors-of-hct-ps)).

Embryo status varies by state. *LePage* (Alabama, February 2024) held that frozen embryos are children under the wrongful-death statute. Alabama's answer, SB159, **gave clinics immunity rather than overturning the reasoning**, which leaves patients with no remedy for embryo loss ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/ivf-caught-crosshairs-aftermath-lepage-decision)). Tennessee, Georgia and Colorado passed right-to-IVF or access laws in 2025 ([MultiState](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2025/11/5/fertility-care-and-ivf-access-in-2025-state-legislation-and-federal-policy-highlights)). Louisiana and Nevada were reported for 2026, but only from a snippet†. The federal Right to IVF Act is unenacted ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9941)). Every state guide should record three things separately: statutory IVF protection, personhood language, and clinic immunity.

Parentage is where CairnFertility's audience carries the most risk. About a dozen states have modern parentage acts (UPA 2017 or equivalent), but that list comes from a low-authority site and needs checking against the Uniform Law Commission ([ULC](https://www.uniformlaws.org/committees/community-home?CommunityKey=c4f37d2d-4d20-4be0-8256-22dd73af068f)). MAP counts **12 states with confirmatory adoption and 26 plus DC with second-parent adoption regardless of marital status**† ([MAP](https://www.lgbtmap.org/equality-maps/second_parent_adoption_laws)). Colorado alone requires identity release at 18 and caps donors at 25 families from 2025 ([Denver7](https://www.denver7.com/news/state-news/new-colorado-law-takes-away-anonymity-of-sperm-and-egg-donors-starting-in-2025)). Michigan legalised compensated surrogacy in 2025, leaving **Nebraska (contracts void) and Louisiana (narrow altruistic only)** as the restrictive outliers ([Varnum](https://www.varnumlaw.com/insights/surrogacy-and-assisted-reproduction-act-passed-in-michigan/)). No federal law stops a clinic refusing single or LGBTQ+ patients. California's *North Coast* ruling shows state civil-rights law doing that work ([FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-supreme-court/1291204.html)). A "welcomes solo and LGBTQ+ patients" flag is valuable, but only when it is verified with the clinic.

## Nobody compares price, success and eligibility for solo and LGBTQ+ patients

The US field is fragmented, and no one serves this niche end to end. SART and CDC publish outcomes without prices or inclusivity information. FertilityIQ, the independent leader, earns from **selling courses to employers** and positions itself as "non-financially-conflicted" ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220201005151/en/FertilityIQ-Records-300-Annual-Growth-100-Client-Retention)†; [Workplace Index](https://www.fertilityiq.com/fertilityiq/articles/fertilityiq-workplace-index)). Men Having Babies runs a 250-provider surrogacy directory for gay dads, funded by provider-sponsored conferences ([MHB](https://menhavingbabies.org/)). The benefit navigators dwarf all of them. **Progyny had $1.29B revenue, 555 clients and about 6.7M members in 2025, but only 65,006 ART cycles**† ([Progyny](https://investors.progyny.com/news-releases/news-release-details/progyny-inc-announces-fourth-quarter-2025-results)). Carrot serves 1,000+ employers ([Carrot](https://www.get-carrot.com/blog/carrot-fertility-provider-expansion-2025)), and Maven was valued at $1.7B ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/10/08/womens-health-startup-maven-clinic-raises-at-1point7-billion-valuation.html)). Their members choose within curated networks. That makes a "my benefit vendor" filter essential, and it leaves the self-pay, small-employer and excluded majority as CairnFertility's natural market. Gaia is the closest UK-to-US precedent. It entered through clinic-group partnerships (57 locations, 23 groups) and a $100M debt facility, not as a content site ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/health-wellness-biotech/ivf-startup-ai-fertility-probability-gaia/)). Bloomberg's September 2025 investigation into Kindbody's add-on sales and the PE consolidation data both strengthen the case for an independent brand ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-26/kindbody-doctors-push-expensive-ivf-procedures-add-ons)).

The money is there. Agency sources put clinic Google CPCs at **about $9.84, rising to $15-$40 in big metros, and cost per lead at about $112**† ([Web Tonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/fertility-ivf-google-ads-statistics)). These are marketing-agency figures, not independent benchmarks. The obvious model, charging clinics per lead or per booking, is also the dangerous one. **Florida's Patient Brokering Act makes any commission or split-fee for patient referrals a crime, whoever pays for the care** ([Fla. Stat. § 817.505](https://flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/817.505)). Carving out federal patients does not immunise an arrangement with a federal nexus under the Anti-Kickback Statute ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20231025-oig-issues-advisory-opinion-on-federal-anti-kickback-statute)). OIG has also warned that a flat fee still has to be fair market value and commercially reasonable ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/office-of-inspector-general-highlights-flat-fee)).

The compliant revenue model follows from that:

1. **Flat-fee, fair-market-value sponsorship or enhanced listings.** Fees are not tied to referrals, leads, bookings or conversions, are labelled "sponsored", and have no effect on rankings or the order of results.
2. **Employer and benefits-broker licensing** of the coverage checker and calculator, following the FertilityIQ route.
3. **MHB-style sponsorship of community events.**

Three things to avoid: percentage-of-enrolment affiliate deals with anything that is itself a provider (for example Dandelion's 10% per enrolled participant, [Dandelion](https://www.dandelionfertility.com/become-an-affiliate)); any payment per lead passed; and any sale of user data. Lender referrals are possible later, but they bring consumer-finance and FTC disclosure obligations.

## California and New York first, Texas and Florida next

Demand is concentrated where coverage is. In 2020, DC plus Connecticut, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey and New York ran above 1.5 times the national per-capita ART rate ([CDC 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/art/state-specific-surveillance/2020/)). The data is old but still indicative. CairnFertility's segments are growing. **9.3% of US adults identified as LGBTQ+ in 2024, and 23.1% of Gen Z**† ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/656708/lgbtq-identification-rises.aspx)). **54% of Cryos customers are single women**, though that is vendor data from a global customer base ([Cryos](https://www.cryosinternational.com/en-us/us-shop/client/donor-sperm/why-use-a-sperm-donor/single-mother-by-choice/)). Egg freezing grew about 39% in 2023 by a secondary count ([Cofertility](https://www.cofertility.com/freeze-learn/state-of-egg-freezing-2025)). SART first recorded partner sex in 2022, which yielded 866 reciprocal IVF transfers for female couples ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12902921/)). That figure undercounts a segment that mostly starts with IUI. Solo mums bridging egg freezing, donor sperm, IUI and IVF are likely the largest addressable pillar. Barriers fall unevenly. **68% of LGBTQ+ respondents said heteronormative definitions blocked care**† ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/why-lgbtq-patients-are-paying-for-gaps-in-fertility-care-284907)). Lower-income women are four times as likely as higher-income women to cite cost (24% vs 6%)† ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/womens-health-policy/access-to-fertility-care-findings-from-the-2024-kff-womens-health-survey/)). Mandates have not narrowed racial gaps ([AJOG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36356698/)).

**California leads** because SB 729 is new, inclusive and confusing, which creates immediate search demand ("does SB 729 cover me?"). It also has the largest LGBTQ+ population and is the surrogacy hub for two-dad families. **New York is second.** It has high utilisation, a dense and crowded clinic market that needs comparing, an existing large-group mandate, and big solo-mum, LGBTQ+ and Jewish communities. Both states have strong civil-rights protection, which lets the site speak confidently to its core audience. California is also the highest-litigation state for privacy (CIPA) and accessibility, so it forces the launch to be clean from day one. **Texas and Florida follow as a second wave.** They are the cost-comparison audience: large, young, heavily Hispanic, and without comprehensive mandates (verify against RESOLVE). They need Spanish content and US faith framing, given the Southern Baptist Convention's 2024 resolution against IVF as commonly practised ([The 19th](https://19thnews.org/2024/06/southern-baptist-convention-ivf-women-pastors/)). Florida enters only once the revenue model has passed counsel review under § 817.505. Illinois, Massachusetts and New Jersey are the third wave, focused on "use my coverage". For channels, most fertility TikTok is patient-made (66.8% by patients, 12.4% by physicians), which leaves room for a neutral explainer voice ([RBMO](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41865501/)). RESOLVE is the natural partner and link target.

## The UK privacy posture is already most of the US answer

HIPAA does not apply to a consumer comparison site that does not act for clinics. The 2024 reproductive-health HIPAA rule was also **vacated nationwide in *Purl v. HHS* (June 2025)**, and the appeal was dropped ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/hipaas-reproductive-health-rule-is-vacated-nationally); [ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/health_law/news/2025/signaling-end-purl-case/)). The real US regime has four parts:

1. **The FTC.** Its amended Health Breach Notification Rule treats unauthorised disclosure as a breach ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2024/04/updated-ftc-health-breach-notification-rule-puts-new-provisions-place-protect-users-health-apps)). Its Section 5 cases against GoodRx ($1.5M), BetterHelp ($7.8M) and the fertility app **Premom** all turned on health data flowing to ad platforms ([FTC GoodRx](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/06/ftc-settles-with-premom-app-developer)).
2. **Washington's MHMDA.** It has no size threshold. It requires separate opt-in consent to collect and to share and a signed authorisation to sell. It requires a Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy with its own homepage link, bans geofences within 2,000 ft of providers, and carries a **private right of action** ([ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/washington-mhmda-updated-guidance-health-data-privacy-policy-requirements/); [Censinet](https://www.censinet.com/perspectives/washington-my-health-my-data-act-key-requirements)). Nevada and Connecticut follow the same pattern. **Maryland's MODPA bans selling sensitive data even with consent**, and enforcement began on 1 April 2026 ([Osano](https://www.osano.com/articles/maryland-online-data-privacy-act-modpa)).
3. **California's AB 254.** It can treat a site that "facilitates" reproductive health services as a CMIA provider ([ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/health-care-counsel-blog/california-adopts-privacy-protections-digital-reproductive)).
4. **CIPA wiretap suits.** A jury found **Meta liable in *Frasco v. Flo Health*** at $5,000 per violation across a class of about 1.5M ([Richt Law](https://richtfirm.com/meta-loses-post-trial-bid-in-landmark-cipa-case-involving-flo-health-app/)). California's SB 690 reform leaves §631 and §632 untouched ([Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/09/californias-sb-690-clears-the-legislature-what-it-means-for-cipa-website-tracking-claims)).

New York's narrowed health-privacy bill (S9269) passed both houses in June 2026 and was awaiting the Governor ([NYS Focus](https://nysfocus.com/2026/03/26/health-data-bill-abortion-digital-surveillance)). With the HIPAA rule gone, nothing federal shields non-HIPAA fertility data from subpoenas. Minimisation is the only durable defence.

The repo already meets most of that bar. The site states "no cookies, no analytics, no advertising or tracking", and the CSP `connect-src 'self'` enforces it in the browser ([src/lib/legal.ts:13-14](../src/lib/legal.ts); [next.config.ts:39-44](../next.config.ts)). Matcher answers stay in browser memory ([src/lib/legal.ts:45-46](../src/lib/legal.ts)), and the gazetteer runs with no geocoder ([src/lib/geo.ts:9-16](../src/lib/geo.ts)). What is missing is US paperwork and consent plumbing:

1. A US privacy notice plus a separate Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy with its own homepage link.
2. Purpose-specific, unticked consent for anything stored or shared, recorded with version and text hash.
3. A `market` column and automatic expiry on the waitlist, which currently holds data "until you unsubscribe" ([src/lib/legal.ts:276](../src/lib/legal.ts)).
4. Rate limiting on a salted, rotating IP hash.
5. `Referrer-Policy` and `Permissions-Policy` headers.
6. An incident plan covering the HBNR (60 days), state breach laws and the ICO (72 hours).
7. A legal-process policy with a transparency report.
8. A WCAG 2.2 AA audit of the wizard and forms. There were 3,117 federal ADA web suits in 2025, led by New York, Florida and California ([Level Access](https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/2024-u-s-web-accessibility-litigation-key-trends-and-strategies-for-mitigating-risk/)).

Two details matter. First, because nothing is tracked, the Global Privacy Control has nothing to opt out of. The site can state that it honours GPC without adding a `proxy.ts` that would break static rendering. Second, Supabase sits in eu-west-1 ([docs/security-review.md:195-201](../docs/security-review.md)). That is lawful for US users, since the UK is not a DOJ "country of concern" ([Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/understanding-and-complying-with-the-dojs-bulk-data-rule/)), and UK GDPR applies to a UK controller anyway. A US project would still simplify both the legal analysis and the trust message. Screen subprocessors for links to countries of concern either way.

## The codebase needs a market key, not a rewrite

The site is UK-first rather than UK-only, and its habit of keeping facts in typed data files under `src/lib/` is its main asset. The blockers are specific:

1. **Prices are stored and filtered in GBP only.** US prices are converted at a single FX note ([src/types/clinic.ts:145-165](../src/types/clinic.ts); [src/lib/clinics.ts:57-68](../src/lib/clinics.ts)).
2. **`Region` has no US value.**
3. **Success-rate verification has only two values, `"hfea" | "clinic"`.** The one US clinic, CCRM Denver, has SART-sourced data that renders as "clinic's own figure, not checked" ([src/lib/clinics.ts:568-606](../src/lib/clinics.ts); [src/lib/rate-labels.ts:8-35](../src/lib/rate-labels.ts)).
4. **The finder's age brackets (38-39, 40-42) do not match SART's (38-40, 41-42)** ([src/types/clinic.ts:10-25](../src/types/clinic.ts)).
5. **US eligibility is a single entry that describes Colorado** ([src/lib/country-eligibility.ts:9-12](../src/lib/country-eligibility.ts)).
6. **Funding is entirely NHS**, and the funding route type has no market field ([src/lib/funding.ts:280-301](../src/lib/funding.ts)).
7. **Legal pages cite UK GDPR and English law**, and `hfeaLicensed` is required on every clinic.
8. **`check:data` would demand a "flight from London" estimate for any US clinic** ([scripts/check-data.ts:305-320](../scripts/check-data.ts)).

The internal strategy brief claims that `funding.ts` already carries country fields. The code does not, so that groundwork has to be redone ([docs/international-strategy-brief.md:231-232](../docs/international-strategy-brief.md)).

Routing should follow the brief. Use **country-scoped paths on the existing canonical `.com`**, with UK at unprefixed paths and a statically generated `/us` segment ([src/lib/site.ts:1-10](../src/lib/site.ts); [docs/international-strategy-brief.md:251-256](../docs/international-strategy-brief.md)). Add `hreflang` en-GB, en-US and x-default, set `<html lang>` per market, and offer a manual market switcher. Any suggestion banner should read `navigator.language` in the browser, never the IP. Avoid geo-IP redirects, because they need a dynamic proxy and break both static caching and the privacy promise. The site is still noindex ([src/app/layout.tsx:24-27](../src/app/layout.tsx)), which makes now the cheapest time to fix the URL scheme. `node_modules` was absent, so the Next 16.3 i18n and proxy guidance in `node_modules/next/dist/docs/` has not yet been read. AGENTS.md requires that before any routing code is written.

## Conclusion

The instinct from the UK is to lead with a clinic league table. In the US that is the weakest opening. SART discourages comparison, CDC publishing has stalled, and ranking by success rates invites FTC scrutiny. The durable asset is the question US patients cannot easily answer: **"given my plan, my state and my family, what will this cost me, and what does the law let me do?"** A plan-type-first coverage checker plus pathway-specific legal guidance is hard for competitors to copy. Navigators serve only their own members, FertilityIQ serves employers, and SART does not touch price or eligibility.

The less obvious finding is that CairnFertility's privacy restraint is a commercial moat as well as a compliance win. US competitors built on pixels and lead fees face CIPA juries, MHMDA plaintiffs and brokering statutes. A site that computes in the browser, sells nothing and charges flat fees can say something they cannot, and in a market shaped by *LePage*, *Purl* and the Flo verdict, that claim is worth more to solo and LGBTQ+ patients than any single feature.
